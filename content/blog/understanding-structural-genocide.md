## A Different Kind of Violence

When people think of genocide, they often picture a single, bounded event — mass killing concentrated in time and place. The final phase of the armed conflict in 2009 was exactly that kind of event. But IDCTE's research focuses on something harder to see: the slower, ongoing processes that continue to reshape the Tamil homeland long after the guns went quiet.

We use the term "structural genocide" to describe this. It refers to state policies — land appropriation, demographic engineering, militarization, and the suppression of Tamil cultural and religious life — that work together over years to erase a people's presence and identity in their own homeland, without a single dramatic event marking their effect.

## Why the Term Matters

Framing matters for accountability. If genocide is understood only as a discrete historical event, it becomes something to commemorate rather than something to stop. IDCTE argues that recognizing the *ongoing* and *structural* nature of the harm is what should drive current policy — sanctions, monitoring, and diplomatic pressure calibrated to a present-tense threat, not only a historical one.

## What We're Tracking

Our research focuses on three interlocking processes: Sinhalization of the North-East's demographics and place names, continued military occupation of civilian land, and restrictions on Tamil commemoration and cultural expression. Each of these findings feeds directly into the briefings we provide policymakers in Geneva, Brussels, and national capitals.
