Today, we pause. Not because pausing is easy — but because fifty years demands it. Fifty years since our people stood at Vaddukoddai and said, with one voice: we exist, we have a right to our future, and we will not be erased.

On May 14, 1976, the Tamil people did something remarkable in its simplicity - **they voted.** Through their elected representatives, gathered at Vaddukoddai in Sri Lanka's Northern Province, they said clearly, peacefully & democratically  that they needed a state of their own. Not because they wanted conflict but because every other option had been taken from them. For decades before that day, Tamil leaders had tried everything. They negotiated. They formed coalitions. They entered pacts with Sinhalese prime ministers. They protested peacefully. They sat in parliament. And every single time, their rights were bargained away, their agreements torn up, their communities left unprotected.

The Vaddukoddai Resolution was not a declaration of war. It was the Tamil nation reaching the end of its patience with a system that had never been designed to include them.

A year later, the Tamil people went to the ballot box and endorsed that resolution overwhelmingly. They followed the rules of democracy — exactly as they were supposed to. And Sri Lanka's response, over the decades that followed, was to make them pay for it.

We want to say something plainly, **because it needs to be said plainly.**

Sri Lanka tells the world it is a democracy. And the world, largely, accepts this.

But **you cannot call yourself a democrac**y while you strip an entire community of citizenship as Sri Lanka did in 1948. You cannot call yourself a democracy while you erase a people's language from public life by law as Sri Lanka did in 1956. 

You cannot call yourself a democracy while your government stands by or worse, participates as thousands of Tamil civilians are killed in the streets, as happened in **Black July, 1983**.

We have watched Sri Lanka perform democracy for the international community for decades. We have watched foreign governments accept that performance because it was convenient to do so. And we have watched the Tamil nation pay the price of that convenience — in displacement, in disappearance, in death.

**We are tired of watching. And we believe the world should be tired of watching too.**
